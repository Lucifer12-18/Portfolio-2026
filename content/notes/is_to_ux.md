Most career pivot stories start with "I was unhappy." Mine doesn't.

I was building web apps at Wipro. The work was concrete, the feedback loop was short, and I was good at it. I wasn't looking for an exit. But I started noticing something that I couldn't stop noticing: the systems I was building had failure modes that only appeared when real humans used them.

Not bugs. Not edge cases. Something harder to define—places where the logic was sound but the experience created friction, confusion, or the wrong outcome for the user. The system worked. The user didn't get what they needed.

I could see the problem clearly. I didn't have the tools to solve it.

That's what pulled me toward design—not dissatisfaction, but a specific gap I wanted to close.

## What Information Systems Actually Taught Me

My master's program at UMBC wasn't design school. It was data flows, system architecture, decision modeling, and how organizations process information. But that framing turned out to be exactly right for the kind of design problems I cared about.

IS gave me one lens: how does data move through a system, where does it transform, and where does it break down?

UX gave me a second lens: where do humans interrupt those data flows, and why?

The interesting territory—the place I actually work—is the intersection. Where a user's mental model of a process collides with how the system actually works.

A concrete example: when I was designing Hirello's networking tier system, the first version was built as a data model. Three tiers: warm contacts, cold contacts, and cold contacts worth warming. The logic was clean. The labels were precise.

Then I ran interviews with job seekers.

Nobody thought in those categories. Users had their own mental models: people they felt comfortable reaching out to, people they felt awkward reaching out to, and people they'd forgotten existed. The data model was right. The categories were wrong.

The fix was renaming and reframing—not rebuilding the architecture. But I wouldn't have known to look there without understanding both the model and the human.

## What a Technical Background Adds

The main thing it adds: you can read the constraint.

When an engineer says "this will add 200ms of latency," I know what that means for the interaction. When a PM says "we can't store that state server-side," I understand the tradeoff we're designing around.

This isn't about being a technical designer. It's about not designing in a vacuum. Constraints aren't obstacles—they're the shape of the problem. If you don't understand them, you end up proposing solutions that collapse the moment engineering looks at them.

More practically: it means engineers trust me. Not because I speak their language perfectly, but because I'm not pretending the constraints don't exist. That trust is more valuable in shipping good work than any specific skill.

## The Frame I Actually Use

I don't think of myself as a designer who came from engineering. I think of myself as someone who learned two languages for the same problem.

Both languages describe how systems should work. Engineering describes the mechanism. Design describes the experience. Neither is complete without the other.

The career path was a translation project. I wasn't abandoning one discipline for another—I was picking up a second vocabulary so I could work at the seam where the hard problems actually live.
